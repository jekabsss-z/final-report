Executive Summary

The strategy holds a fixed 35% world-equity anchor and rotates the remaining 65% between one sector sleeve (30%) and one factor sleeve (35%), selected from each sleeve's historical performance in the prevailing macro regime. Selection is strictly point-in-time — only data available at the decision date is used.

Over 2001–2026 it returned 9.0% annualised versus 7.5% for MSCI World, at a higher Sharpe ratio (0.45 vs 0.36) and a shallower maximum drawdown (−51% vs −54%). The excess return is concentrated in weak regimes rather than spread evenly across the sample.

Methodology

Regimes & Selection

Four Growth×Inflation states — Recovery, Goldilocks / Mid-cycle, Late-cycle / Stagflation, Contraction / Recession — from six macro indicators (three growth, one inflation, two liquidity / financial-conditions), with a two-month persistence filter. Each month every sleeve is scored by its average return within the current regime, computed on prior months only, and the top sector and factor are selected.

Weighting & Backtest

MSCI World 35% + Sector 30% + Factor 35%

Monthly weighted returns compound into a growth-of-100 index and are compared with MSCI World Net TR. Rebalanced monthly, fully invested, gross of costs.

Key Findings

  1. Regime conditioning improves return and risk. +1.5 pp of annualised return over the benchmark with a higher Sharpe ratio and a smaller maximum drawdown.
  2. Excess return concentrates in weak regimes. Across 142 Late-cycle and Contraction months the strategy compounded ≈+75% versus +16% for the benchmark; benign regimes tracked the market.
  3. Leadership is regime-dependent. High-beta cyclicals lead Recovery and the rebound; defensives limit losses in Late-cycle. A single all-period winner misses this rotation.
  4. Contraction is positive because markets lead the economy. The deepest drawdowns fall one regime earlier, in Late-cycle (MSCI World −6%); by the time data confirm contraction, equities are already recovering — an early-recovery rally (IT +16%, Small Cap +13%, Discretionary +12%).
06

Methodological Lessons

  1. Look-ahead bias overstated results. Selecting from the full sample and testing on it gave 11.8% / 0.62; the point-in-time rebuild fell to 9.0% / 0.45. The ~3 pp gap is unrealisable hindsight.
  2. Return conventions must be consistent. Returns are stored simple; a stray log transform (EXP()−1) distorted every downstream figure. Portfolio returns aggregate only on simple returns.
  3. Turnover is a controllable cost. A three-month minimum hold raised average holding length to ≈6 months and cut trading sharply, at ≈1 pp of CAGR (8.7% vs 9.8%).
  4. Sparse regimes need explicit handling. A regime's first appearance has no prior data; scores use error-handling with a defined fallback so nothing errors or blanks.
  5. Auditability over complexity. Fixed documented weights and one calculation sheet feeding backtest and metrics keep the model transparent.

Recommended Next Steps

  • Out-of-sample validation — derive weights on an earlier window, evaluate on a later one.
  • Transaction costs — apply a per-switch cost; the 3-month hold should lead net.
  • Parameter sensitivity — vary weight split, hold period and persistence to confirm stability.
  • Live signal — current reading (Goldilocks; Energy + Momentum) updates as new data arrive.
09

Bottom-Line Takeaways

  1. It's a downside-timing engine, not an alpha machine. The ≈+1.5 pp per year comes from holding the right defensives before the drop and the right cyclicals into the recovery — not from being cleverer in good markets.
  2. The Late-cycle → Contraction transition is where it's won or lost. Roughly all of the outperformance is earned across those regimes; everything else roughly tracks the index.
  3. Set honest expectations. ≈9% a year with equity-like risk and a −51% worst case — it still falls hard in a bear market. This is a smarter equity allocation, not a hedge.
  1. Simplicity was enough. A fixed 35 / 30 / 35 rule beat the benchmark with no optimisation — any added complexity should have to clear a high bar before it goes in.
  2. The real deployment risks are regime misclassification and turnover. For live use, favour the three-month hold and treat the 9.0% as an in-sample upper bound until it survives an out-of-sample test.
